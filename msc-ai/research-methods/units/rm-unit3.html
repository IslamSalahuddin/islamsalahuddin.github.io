Research Methods - Unit 3: Methodology and Research Methods

Overview

As per the course website, "Welcome to week 3, where you will be introduced to the core of any research: the methodology. It is crucial to understand that any methodology choice is based on some underlying assumptions, which we need to bring awareness to. As Saunders et al. (2023) clearly states, you, the researcher, ‘are going to have assumptions about the realities you encounter in your research (ontological assumptions), about human knowledge (epistemological assumptions), and about the extent and ways your own values influence your research process (axiological assumptions). These assumptions inevitably shape how you understand your research questions, the methods you use and how you interpret your findings.’"

My Reflection

Overall Reflection

Similar to the previous unit, I'm still working to catch up on the course's progress, documenting this unit in Week 5 instead of Week 3.

The unit had a lecturecast and a long list of readings and video materials focused on methodology and research methods. The ones that were most interesting out of those readings are chapters on problem solving, formulation of research problems and research proposals from a textbook called Management Research Methodology by Krishnaswamy, Sivakumar and Mathirajan (2006), as well as a chapter on formulating the research design from another textbook called Research Methods for Business Students by Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill (2023). Both ebooks are core text books for the module.


Reading Reflection

The chapter by Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill (2023) covered not only research methods that are classical and well known, it also covered others that I did know much about before and that I found worthy to make note of. For example, it was intriguing to see some classifications for ethnography, like realist, interpretive and critical ethnography. Autoethnography was specially interesting for me because I realised this is very close to the way I've been thinking and working on my creative writing projects for the last few years, and yet I knew nothing about. So, I decided to study it further later on.

In the same chapter, I also read about narrative inquiry, which was one of the methods that were interesting for the reason that it gives a space to, and acknowledges, subjectivity of experiences within the academic realm, as opposed to considering this subjectivity as non-factual or non-academic. Related to this, it was also the first time to encounter a related method called "artistic inquiry", which mixes art and research for art-based research, through another interactive map on research methods by Sage. As I was familiar already with research-based art among the art communities, I found out that the other way around exists as well. Research Methods for Business Students also introduced me to the concepts of sing and multiple case studies, action research and Grounded Theory.

Grounded Theory was something that Krishnaswamy, Sivakumar and Mathirajan (2006) covered as well, in addition to worthy notes around different methods for problem solving, and where creative thinking fits in the overall picture of problem solving in both practical and academic contexts. I took notes on Hutchinson creatove problem solving process (1961), principles of creative development by Parnes (1964) and motives for creative thinking by Saaty (1959), all of which I'd love to read about in the future. Additionally, below are some of the highlights that I liked from reading the chapters by Krishnaswamy, Sivakumar and Mathirajan (2006):

  • Comprehension is necessary but not sufficient for creativity.
  • Severe regimentation stifles creative talent but balanced guidance could be given through goals and objectives.
  • The absence of pathways in the brain is a barrier to creative thinking. It can, however, be overcome by recognising the difficulty and by studying the subject deeply. Incorrect data, incorrect direct sensory impressions, and incorrect associations cause pathways as barriers. All human beings have incorrect thought patterns on broad subjects, and they try to study some limited aspects of the system and engage in generalisations within the system. Experience, the noblest school with heavy fees, may also be a block to creative thinking.
  • Relationships change with time but time is not involved in the change. Many natural relationships change as the degree of one’s experience of it changes. In this context, experience must be viewed with caution. Ignorance helps in thinking creatively in relatively well known areas. The creative thinker should (a) constantly examine the relationship to be sure that it is not changing, (b) identify as sources of errors, the change in the composition of groups or collection of objects, or should notice the degree of change in one of the objects, or should notice that the same relationship is different in various systems, and (c) synthesise and put together relationships in a new combination.
  • A thought is an interpretation of the relationship existing in the external universe. While there is a mechanism to generate thoughts, there is no mechanism in the brain to distinguish the correct thought from the incorrect one, that is, to distinguish the correct interpretation of the true relationships from the wrong interpretation.
  • A good hypothesis must be conceptually clear, empirically based, must be specific in order to facilitate testing, and must be deduced from theory.
  • The specificity can be evaluated by seeking whether it can be tested quantitatively using operational definitions. If necessary, a hypothesis can be split up into sub-hypotheses, which are more specific.
  • He can divide/partition the research literature into three broad categories. Firstly, general literature in the broad area of specialisation has to be browsed quickly, noting only the portion that may have a bearing on the problem area. Secondly, literature related to the problem area in which research is being carried out. This has to be studied intensely and a bibliography may be developed. Summaries of some of the designs adopted, measurements made, and analysis methods and findings may be generated. The gaps must be identified (if the problem is not already coarsely formulated).
