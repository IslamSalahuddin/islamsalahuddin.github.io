Research Methods - Unit 5: Interviews, Survey Methods, and Questionnaire Design

Overview

As per the course website, "Welcome to Week 5 where you will be introduced to the use of interviews, survey methods and questionnaire design. Conducting in-depth interviews is one of the most common qualitative research methods. It is a conversational piece, involving only respondent at a time. It provides opportunities for the researcher to get in depth details from the respondent. A survey is one of the most important quantitative methods of collecting data. There are various types of surveys or polls, and they are used to explore opinions, trends, etc. Online surveys are more generally used, with easier accessibility for the target respondents."

My Reflection

Overall Reflection

I'm catching up on this unit in Week 6, feeling close to having covered what I missed and therefore, hopefully, having more time to start working on Research Design Proposal due at the end of the module. Unit 5 included a lecturecast, a list of readings and a couple of formative activities. Through the list of readings, I learnt a few things related to the topic of the module.

One of the things that I learned in this unit is that there is a difference between a survey and a questionnaire. While the latter refers to the actual data collection tool that is the list of the questions, the former refers to the overarching process of designing, building, dissemenating, analysing and interpreting a questionnaire and its results. A questionnaire can be used with surveys, as well as other methods, like interviews and experiments, while on the other hand a survey always include a questionnaire.

One example that is relevant to me is that I'm thinking of implementing an experiment in my thesis, and the way that I'm envisioning this experiment has participants mainly answering questions on a computer. Reading about what questionnaires are and how they are different from surveys, I realises that this would be a questionnaire-based experiment. However, with a bit of search, I found that there exists nothing as such and the closest method is called a survey experiment. I'm still not sure if this is the right name for the method that I'm thinking of.

In a book titled Questionnaire Design: How to Plan, Structure and Write Survey Material for Effective Market Research by Brace (2013), I read about how an interviewer -if exists- might alter the direction of a questionnaire by subtle rephrasing, and how "I don't know" answers may be useful in many cases. I also read about the different types of misleading questions, like loaded, double-barreled and ambiguous questions, which were covered by other reading materials as well as the lecturecast. Also in the book, I read that closed questions are good to be simple with clear-cut answers. So, I started thinking about one question in my experiment that I enviisioned with four answer choices, while I'm interested to later categorise under two labels, and therefore started wondering whether two answer choices would be more suitable if paraphrased correctly. I believe I will need to discuss this further with the module's tutor or the supervisor of the thesis.

Formative Activities

Inappropriate Use of Surveys

The formative activity highlihted two cases of inappropriate use of surveys, inviting students to reflect on where they drifted and what to learn from them.

The first case was Cambridge Analytica in 2018, the political consultancy firm that collected data from million of Facebook users, without informed consent of using the data for political and monetary purposes. Cambridge Analytica was also accused of exploiting platform vulnerabilities and possible manipulation of political processes (Raychaudhuri, 2025). Overall, this involved many ethical complications, like violation of users autonomy and consent, hijacking political processes and shaking social trust in rearch, in addition to breaching data protection laws.

The second case was University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Survey, which was not an ethical scandal as much it was a methodology-related problematic case of how changing the medium of data collection may break the data continuity and connection.

The university switched from phone to online as a medium for conducting their surveys, causing significantly lower sentiment reading - around an 8.9-point drop. The new medium was less capable of taking mode effects into consideration and therefore introduced bias to the historical continuity of the collected data (Leer, 2024).

Two Additional Examples

The activity also asked students to search for and think about two additional examples. Below are the ones I chose:

Google Flu Trends (2013)

Google Flu Trends was launched in 2008 as an ambitious project to predict flu outbreaks in real time usig People's Google search queries statistical correlations between search terms and flu activity (Lazer et al., 2014). The idea was that if lots of people in a region suddenly search for "flu symptoms", "fever remedies" or "cough medecine", flu cases are probably rising there, aiming to outperform traditional epidemiological surveillance.

Despite that is not a survey in the exact since, I chose this as an example, because it also involves using data for purposes other than the ones that it was collected (and generated for) like the case of Cambridge Analytica, in addition to a problem with the methodology that lead to its failure in 2013 and termination in 2015. The point of failure was when the model overestimated the 2013 flu activity by nearly 140%.

There were several methodological problems with the model that include the following:

  • Over reliance on digital traces as evidence for illness and epidemic outbreak.
  • Not accounting for system innate biases, like media coverage that inflates trends and the autocomplete suggestions of the search engine itself.
  • Lack of transparency, as Google never declared the search terms that the model was built on not its other intricate details.
  • Ignoring established surveillance systems global monitoring reports, hospitals admission data and laboratory confirmed cases, aiming to replace the existing system, instead of complimenting and benefiting from it.

The Literary Digest Poll (1936)

A way more traditional example is the 1936 poll of the Literary Digest Magazine of the United States that conducted a wide political survey sending out 10 miliion questionnaires, receiving 2.4 million respones, predicting a landslide victory for Alf Londing over Franklin D. Roosevelt before the actual result coming out being the exact opposite (Lusinchi, 2012).

The mistake here was mainly a sampling bias, with the magazine assuming that its readers represent the entire US voters population, while its target audience were generally wealthier than the average American during that time.
